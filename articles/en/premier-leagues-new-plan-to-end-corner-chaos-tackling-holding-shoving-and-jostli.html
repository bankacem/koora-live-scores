Premier League's New Plan to End Corner Chaos: Tackling Holding, Shoving and Jostling

8/20/2026 · By Koora Live

BBC Sport has reported that the Premier League has drawn up a new plan to tackle what has become an epidemic of rough contact at corner kicks. Holding, pushing, shoving, jostling and grappling at set-pieces were highlighted as a widespread problem last season, and the league is now seeking ways to address those issues.

The story from BBC Sport has reignited debate over how set-pieces are officiated and how teams coach defensive and attacking routines. Below we explain the nature of the problem, common ways governing bodies can respond, and what this might mean for clubs, referees and fans — framed against widely accepted rules and practices in the game.

What do we mean by ‘corner chaos’?

The phrase refers to the frequent and often aggressive physical contact that breaks out in the penalty area when a corner is delivered. Actions described by BBC Sport — holding, pushing, shoving, jostling and grappling — are all types of contact that can interfere with a player’s ability to move, jump or play the ball. Under the Laws of the Game, many of these actions can constitute fouls if they unfairly impede an opponent.

Corners are inherently congested moments. Multiple attackers and defenders crowd a small space, all vying for position as the ball arrives. That density, combined with the high stakes of set-pieces, makes them fertile ground for physical contests which officials must police. When such behaviour becomes routine rather than occasional, pundits and officials begin to use terms like ‘chaos’ to describe the situation.

Why it became a season-long concern and how leagues typically respond

According to BBC Sport, the level of contact at corners reached epidemic proportions last season — meaning the issue was widespread and repeatedly observed across matches. When a pattern like this emerges, leagues and refereeing bodies have several established responses at their disposal. Common remedial actions include clearer enforcement by match officials, updated guidance or interpretations of existing laws, targeted training for referees on set-piece situations, and the use of video review systems to back up on-field decisions.

None of those responses are novel: football authorities have previously tweaked interpretations and provided referees with directive notes after repetitive problems. Clubs also play a role by adjusting coaching methods and set-piece discipline. Any effective solution tends to be a combination of on-field enforcement, off-field guidance for referees, and behavioural changes from coaching staff and players.

Analysis

If the Premier League implements measures to reduce rough contact at corners, the most immediate effect would likely be on how referees manage set-pieces. Stricter enforcement could reduce cynical defending and reward cleaner attacking movement, altering the tactical value of short and conventional corner routines. However, any change will require consistent application: players and coaches adapt quickly when they identify what referees will or won’t allow. Analysts might see this as a longer-term cultural shift rather than a quick fix — referees, clubs and governing bodies need to work in concert to change entrenched behaviours.

BBC Sport’s coverage has shone a spotlight on a persistent set-piece problem in the Premier League. While the league’s new plan aims to curb the rougher side of corner kicks, meaningful change will depend on consistent refereeing, clear guidance and behavioural adjustments from teams. Fans should expect an ongoing conversation as officials and clubs work through practical ways to restore order to one of football’s most volatile moments.
