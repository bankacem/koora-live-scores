Who Could Replace FIFA President Gianni Infantino? Explaining the Process and Potential Profiles

8/3/2026 · By Koora Live

BBC Sport has flagged mounting pressure on FIFA president Gianni Infantino and posed a simple but consequential question: if he goes, who comes in? The prospect of change at the top of world football's governing body prompts immediate practical questions about the mechanics of succession and the kinds of figures who might be viable candidates. This article explains how a FIFA presidency is decided, the types of contenders that historically emerge, and what stakeholders typically look for in a successor.

How a FIFA presidential election works

The FIFA president is chosen by the FIFA Congress, the organisation's supreme decision-making body composed of national associations. Each member association carries one vote, and presidential elections are held at Congress under the procedures laid out in FIFA's statutes. Candidates must meet eligibility criteria set by FIFA and usually need nomination support from national associations to stand.

Elections are typically timed to the body's calendar and can align with its four-year cycles that coincide with World Cup planning, though the precise arrangements for any given vote depend on FIFA's regulations and the Congress agenda. If a sitting president resigns or is otherwise unable to continue, FIFA's statutes provide for transitional arrangements and for an extraordinary or regular Congress to elect a successor.

What kinds of candidates tend to emerge

Historically, successful presidential candidates have come from several broad backgrounds. Many are senior figures with long experience inside football governance — presidents or executives of continental confederations or influential national associations. Others have combined governance experience with diplomatic skills, legal or commercial expertise, and the ability to navigate international politics.

A viable candidate typically needs a combination of name recognition across regions, the ability to build alliances among the 211 member associations, and a policy platform that addresses pressing issues within football — from integrity and transparency to development and commercial partnerships. Regional balance, language skills and relationships with confederation leaders can also be decisive in the multi-round voting processes used in past FIFA contests.

Analysis

BBC Sport's framing that "pressure is building" on Gianni Infantino makes the question of succession unavoidable for observers. If a vacancy were to occur, the race would likely be shaped as much by behind-the-scenes coalition-building as by public policy platforms. Analysts might see any contest as a test of modern football's governance structures: whether member associations prioritise continuity and commercial stability or opt for reform and accountability.

Whatever the scenario, a replacement would need to secure wide geographic support. Past FIFA transitions have shown that no single region can determine an outcome alone; successful contenders typically assemble a cross-confederation coalition. The dynamics of such an election would also reflect wider debates in the sport about governance, financial distribution and the calendar — issues that tend to define presidential campaigns.

The question posed by BBC Sport — if Infantino goes, who comes in? — opens a complex conversation about governance, regional politics and the qualities needed to lead world football. Any replacement will have to navigate FIFA's institutional processes and assemble a broad coalition of support across the sport's global membership.
