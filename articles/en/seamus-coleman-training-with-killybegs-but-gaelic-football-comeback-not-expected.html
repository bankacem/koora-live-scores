Seamus Coleman training with Killybegs but Gaelic football comeback not expected yet

8/9/2026 · By Koora Live

Former Everton defender Seamus Coleman has been seen training with his local Gaelic football club Killybegs, according to a report by BBC Sport. While the sessions mark a return to his sporting roots, a full switch back to club-level Gaelic football is not expected at this time.

What BBC Sport has reported

BBC Sport has reported that Seamus Coleman, described in their coverage as a former Everton defender, has taken part in training with Killybegs, his local Gaelic football club. The same report indicates that, despite those training appearances, a formal return to playing for the GAA club does not appear likely in the immediate future.

This coverage provides a clear snapshot: Coleman has engaged with his local Gaelic football environment, but that engagement should not be read as a confirmed switch back to the sport at club level.

Context: Coleman, Killybegs and the crossover between association football and Gaelic football

Seamus Coleman is widely known for his long association with professional association football; the BBC Sport piece identifies him as a former defender for Everton. Killybegs is his local Gaelic football club, part of the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA) structure that governs Gaelic games across Ireland. It is common for players from communities with dual-sport traditions to maintain connections to their local clubs even after establishing themselves in professional soccer.

Training with a hometown GAA club can mean a range of things: staying fit in the off-season, enjoying community involvement, or simply reconnecting with the game where a player first developed. Any consideration of a competitive comeback at club level would involve factors such as fitness, availability, family commitments and the differing demands of association football and Gaelic football — all of which are common issues when athletes contemplate switching codes.

Coleman's involvement in training sessions with Killybegs is emblematic of the strong ties many Irish footballers retain with their local GAA clubs. For supporters and community members, seeing a high-profile athlete back at a grassroots pitch can be a boost for local morale and youth participation. From a sporting perspective, occasional training does not necessarily signal a long-term shift; the physical and scheduling demands of GAA club football differ from association football, and players weighing a return usually factor in how it fits with their wider professional and personal priorities.

Analysts might interpret the BBC Sport report as a signal that Coleman is keeping options open while prioritising measured involvement, rather than committing to a permanent code change. For local supporters, the immediate benefit is the presence and influence of an experienced athlete in training, even if competitive appearances remain unlikely for now.
