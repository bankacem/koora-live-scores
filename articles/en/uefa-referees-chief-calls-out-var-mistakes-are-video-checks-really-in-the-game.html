UEFA Referees Chief Calls Out VAR Mistakes: Are Video Checks Really in the Game?

8/10/2026 · By Koora Live

In a bold statement that has rippled through the football community, Roberto Rosetti, head of the UEFA refereeing department, told the BBC that some video assistant referee reviews were "not connected with football". His comment highlights growing frustration with the technology that is supposed to protect the sport’s integrity. Over the past years, VAR has promised clarity but has also drawn criticism for inconsistent application and delayed decisions. Rossi’s assertion demystifies the debate and forces clubs, officials, and fans to reevaluate how much reliance should be placed on machine‑assisted judgments.

The Rise and Limits of VAR

Var was introduced in 2016 as a support system for referees, allowing them to review clear and obvious errors on four critical decision categories: goals, penalties, direct red cards, and cases of mistaken identity. Since then, more than 500,000 checks have taken place across tournaments worldwide, from domestic leagues to the Champions League and Euro finals. Technically, the system boasts high‑resolution cameras and video footage fed into a real‑time decision console. Yet the human element remains central: the on‑field referee accepts the review, the VAR team discusses, and they return a decision, often after a pause in play. The process was designed to reduce error while balancing the flow of the game, but the subjective nature of the criteria can still generate uproar.

Rosetti’s Frustrating Observation

During a UEFA press briefing, Rosetti remarked that certain video reviews felt disconnected from what actually takes place on the pitch. He stressed that the core of football is about the action unfolding in real time, and that technology should serve to clarify, not obscure, the moment. While he did not list specific matches, the implication is clear: when the VAR verdict is based on a camera angle that misrepresents a play, the decision can feel alienated from the game’s context. This sentiment aligns with complaints from referees who occasionally find evidence that favors a controversial outcome, thereby amplifying perceived injustice.

Balancing Technology and Human Judgment

The debate over VAR is a microcosm of a larger tension in sports: how to harness artificial intelligence without losing the art of officiating. The referee’s own authority is challenged when a video replay, arguably more detailed, overrides their instinct. Conversely, the technology offers a safety net against obvious mistakes that could change a World Cup or Champions League final. Analysts suggest that procedural reforms—such as clearer guidelines on what constitutes a clear error—could mitigate the disconnect. Implementing a minimum review time or a broader review window ensures that the decision is contextualised both visually and contextually.

The Reaction of Clubs, Players, and Fans

Clubs have invested heavily in referee partnerships, hoping that VAR will deliver fairness. Players, however, have sometimes expressed frustration that their own wrongdoing is overturned after the fact. Fans, especially in the digital age, can watch live footage of the same incident and judge the decision’s validity. The public scrutiny has prompted UEFA to revisit the VAR play‑by‑play system, including closer coordination between the match officials and the VAR team. In some leagues, clubs have requested more elaborate review stations or second‑look options, recognising that the technology still has room to evolve.

Analysis

Rosetti’s comments highlight a systemic challenge: VAR’s two‑tier architecture can sometimes separate the decision tree from the moment it aims to correct. If the evidence reviewed does not accurately reflect the set‑up or context, the service fails its purpose of safeguarding the game. A more integrated approach—where the on‑field referee’s timeline is mirrored exactly in the VAR feed—may create a more coherent decision‑making pathway. Nevertheless, the technology’s core benefit remains: providing a net of human support that can correct glaring mistakes that would otherwise irreparably alter outcomes.

As football continues to blend tradition with innovation, the conversation around VAR underscores the need for technology that truly complements human judgment. By acknowledging and addressing disconnections highlighted by officials like Roberto Rosetti, UEFA and the broader football community can move toward an officiating system that both respects the spirit of the game and delivers the clarity fans demand.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does VAR stand for and what is its purpose?

VAR means Video Assistant Referee. The system is designed to help match officials correct clear and obvious errors in the four key categories—goals, penalties, direct red cards, and mistaken identity—by reviewing footage in real time.

What did Roberto Rosetti say about VAR reviews?

Rosetti, the chief of UEFA referees, stated that certain VAR reviews seemed disconnected from actual football action, implying that they did not always reflect the true nature of on‑field events.

Why has VAR faced criticism among clubs and fans?

Clubs and fans argue that the review process can become subjective, delay games, and at times appear to favour one side when the evidence reviewed misrepresents the play. Such outcomes can erode trust in the refereeing system, prompting calls for clearer protocols and better integration with on‑field actions.
