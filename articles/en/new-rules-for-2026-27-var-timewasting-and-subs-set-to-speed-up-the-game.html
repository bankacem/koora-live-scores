New rules for 2026-27: VAR, timewasting and subs set to speed up the game

8/14/2026 · By Koora Live

BBC Sport has reported that the 2026-27 season will bring an unprecedented number of new laws, with the headline focus on VAR, timewasting and substitutions. According to the report, the changes are mostly designed to tackle tempo disruption and make the game quicker. This represents one of the most significant regulatory refreshes in recent memory for professional football.

Why tempo and match flow have become priority issues

In modern football, controlling the rhythm of a match is both a tactical tool and a spectator concern. Timewasting — broadly understood as deliberate actions to delay restarts, slow the game or disrupt an opponent's momentum — has been part of the sport for generations. In recent years, however, governing bodies and broadcasters have increasingly highlighted the cumulative effect of repeated delays on match entertainment, broadcast schedules and player welfare. Adjustments to the laws of the game are one lever used by rule-makers to preserve a cleaner, more continuous spectacle.

Review systems such as VAR (video assistant referee) have also reshaped game flow. While VAR aims to correct clear and obvious errors, its use has sometimes extended stoppages and provoked debate about balance between accuracy and continuity. Substitutions are another tactical instrument affecting tempo: managers can use changes to manage fatigue, protect leads or break opponent patterns. Any coordinated set of law changes that targets VAR procedures, timewasting and substitution protocols is therefore likely intended to influence how tightly the game’s clock and rhythm are managed.

What clubs, players and fans should expect in broad terms

Though the exact provisions reported by BBC Sport are not detailed here, stakeholders can reasonably expect that the package of changes will seek to reduce preventable stoppages and encourage continuous play. Historically, law changes have included measures such as stricter enforcement of existing rules, clarifications to reduce subjective interpretations, or procedural tweaks to speed up decision-making. Clubs and coaching staffs typically need a period of adjustment when multiple laws change at once, as training, match management and substitution strategies are adapted.

Fans and broadcasters are likely to monitor how alterations affect viewing experience. Referees, supported by referee associations and professional trainers, will be central to implementing any new directives consistently. If the aim is to make the game quicker, match officials will need clear guidance and communication tools so that changes are applied uniformly across competitions.

Analysis

The reported package of changes — described by BBC Sport as unprecedented in number — signals a concerted effort by law-makers to prioritise continuous play. Analysts might see this as an attempt to reconcile the competing demands of fairness (through tools like VAR) and entertainment (through a faster tempo). Clubs that adapt quickly to clearer protocols and coaching teams that rehearse new in-game routines could gain marginal advantages. Ultimately, consistent enforcement will be the decisive factor in whether these changes materially shorten matches or simply shift how delays are executed.

The BBC Sport report highlights a notable shift in football law-making ahead of the 2026-27 season, with VAR, timewasting and substitutions at the centre of efforts to speed up play. As clubs, officials and fans prepare for these adjustments, the practical impact will depend on how clearly the new measures are communicated and consistently enforced across competitions.

Why would football governing bodies change laws on VAR, timewasting and substitutions?

Governing bodies typically adjust laws to improve fairness, clarity and the spectator experience. VAR relates to decision accuracy but can extend stoppages; timewasting addresses deliberate delays that harm match flow; and substitutions influence momentum and player management. Changes in these areas aim to reduce unnecessary interruptions and encourage a smoother, faster game.

Will these law changes apply across all competitions immediately?

Implementation of law changes usually follows formal approval by the relevant rule-making authorities and then a timetable for adoption by competitions. Different leagues and tournaments may adopt new laws according to that schedule, and referees and clubs typically receive guidance and training before full implementation.
