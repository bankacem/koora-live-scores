David Moyes Reveals Zero Contact with Everton Owner Dan Friedkin

9/5/2026 · By Koora Live

Everton manager David Moyes has made a surprising revelation, stating that he has never met or engaged in conversation with the club's owner, Dan Friedkin. This admission has sparked curiosity over the managerial dynamics behind the scenes at the historic club.

Moyes Shares Surprising Insight on Club Ownership

In a candid moment, David Moyes has stated that he has not had any personal communication with Everton owner Dan Friedkin. This unexpected revelation raises questions about the interaction between the managerial and ownership levels at one of England's oldest professional football clubs. Established in 1878, Everton has a storied tradition, and its ownership structure has historically played a key role in the club's direction and strategy. However, Moyes' comment places a spotlight on an apparent lack of direct rapport between the manager and the club's top decision-maker.

Everton and David Moyes: A Complex Relationship

David Moyes returned to Everton under much anticipation, having previously built a strong reputation during his first spell in charge from 2002 to 2013. He is widely credited with leading the team to consistent finishes in the top half of the Premier League, despite budgetary constraints against wealthier competitors. Under Moyes, the Toffees became a steady force in England's top flight, with his managerial vision forming the basis of their successes during that period. However, his assertion about never having spoken to Dan Friedkin may hint at potential operational silos within the club. With modern football increasingly requiring tight alignment between ownership and coaching staff, the revelation could prompt pointed queries about internal communication at Everton.

Analysis

Moyes' comment about never having spoken with Dan Friedkin could be interpreted as indicative of a larger distance between the managerial and ownership levels at Everton. In an era where football clubs are run as intricate businesses—not only sporting institutions—close collaboration between managers and owners is often viewed as essential for long-term success. While some might argue that giving a manager full autonomy is a sign of trust in his or her abilities, others may see the lack of direct communication as a missed opportunity for alignment on crucial club matters, ranging from transfer strategies to long-term organizational goals. This revelation is likely to reignite debates about governance in modern football.
